Understanding Your Result

What is Body Mass Index (BMI) and how is it calculated?

One number — many ways to be well

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a widely used, practical screening tool used by UK healthcare providers, including the NHS, to check whether an adult’s weight is broadly proportionate to their height. Rather than measuring body fat directly, BMI estimates weight relative to stature using a standardised mathematical ratio.

Metric Formula

Divide your weight in kilograms ($\text{kg}$) by your height in metres ($\text{m}$) squared:

$\text{BMI} = \text{weight (kg)} \div [\text{height (m)}]^2$

Imperial Formula

Convert stones to pounds ($1\text{ st} = 14\text{ lb}$) and feet to inches ($1\text{ ft} = 12\text{ in}$):

$\text{BMI} = [\text{weight (lb)} \div \text{height (in)}^2] \times 703$

Because muscle tissue is denser than fat tissue, people with a naturally muscular build—such as weightlifters, rugby players, or manual labourers—may record a BMI in the overweight range despite having low body fat. Equally, older adults who have lost muscle mass over time may record a healthy BMI while carrying higher internal fat levels.

NHS Reference Ranges

How to interpret adult BMI categories in the UK

In the United Kingdom, adult BMI classifications are grouped into four main bands. Evidence from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) shows that adults from South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African, or African-Caribbean family backgrounds face an increased risk of conditions such as type 2 diabetes at lower BMI thresholds.

Category Standard Adult Range Adjusted Thresholds (Select Backgrounds) General Guidance
Underweight Below 18.5 Below 18.5 May indicate insufficient calorie intake or underlying health needs; consider speaking with a GP.
Healthy weight 18.5 to 24.9 18.5 to 22.9 Associated with lower long-term health risks; maintain balanced nutrition and regular movement.
Overweight 25.0 to 29.9 23.0 to 27.4 Gradual, sustainable adjustments to daily activity and portion balance can help lower metabolic risk.
Obesity 30.0 or above 27.5 or above Higher risk of cardiovascular and metabolic conditions; NHS weight management support is available.
Support for your next step

Wellbeing, made practical

For a more complete picture of your overall health, UK clinicians often look at BMI alongside other practical measurements such as your waist-to-height ratio (aiming to keep your waist measurement to less than half your height), blood pressure, cholesterol levels, and daily lifestyle habits.

Please note that standard adult BMI ranges are intended for adults aged 18 and over and are not suitable for children, teenagers, or women who are pregnant. If you have questions about your weight or health goals, speak with a GP, community pharmacist, or registered dietitian for tailored advice.

Common Questions

Frequently asked questions about BMI

Is my height and weight data stored or shared? +

No. All BMI calculations on BMIBMI happen directly inside your own web browser. We do not collect, store, or transmit the height or weight numbers you enter into our calculator.

Why does the NHS recommend measuring waist size alongside BMI? +

BMI measures total weight relative to height, whereas waist-to-height ratio checks how much fat is carried around your middle (visceral fat). Carrying excess weight around the abdomen is more closely linked to heart health and type 2 diabetes risk than weight carried on the hips or thighs.

Can children or teenagers use this BMI calculator? +

No. This tool uses adult thresholds intended for people aged 18 and older. Because children and young people are still growing, their BMI is assessed using age- and sex-specific growth centile charts, such as the official NHS Children’s BMI calculator.

What is the difference between Imperial (stones/pounds) and Metric (kg/cm)? +

Both systems produce the exact same BMI score. In the UK, many people measure weight in stones and pounds ($1\text{ stone} = 14\text{ pounds} \approx 6.35\text{ kg}$) and height in feet and inches, while GP surgeries typically record height in centimetres and weight in kilograms. You can use whichever side feels most familiar.
